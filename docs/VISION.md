# Product Vision

## Experience statement

The player governs a historical polity in an ancient world that is recognizable at first glance but altered by rare, dangerous, and culturally specific forms of magic. The campaign should feel broader than the screen: distant courts make decisions, roads and rivers carry trade and armies, settlements develop over generations, border peoples remember old grievances, and local missions can change regional politics.

The game is turn-based at every authoritative layer, but resolution may be animated continuously. A strategic month can contain diplomacy, construction, recruitment, movement, discovery, covert work, and battles. A battle phase can show thousands of soldiers moving while the underlying authority remains formations and discrete resolution steps. A party encounter gives individual characters tactical agency.

## Design pillars

### 1. Vast through consequence, not empty acreage

Scale comes from interconnected systems and persistent consequences. A smaller number of meaningful provinces is better than a huge grid of indistinguishable cells. Roads, food, loyalty, culture, command, weather, trade, and information should make geography matter.

### 2. Armies are institutions

An army is not merely a stack of attack values. It has formations, officers, traditions, supply, morale, fatigue, baggage, scouting, recruitment sources, and political weight. Its soldiers appear on the battlefield, but its important decisions occur at formation and command level.

### 3. Two tactical lenses

Formation battles and party encounters share characters, equipment concepts, status effects, terrain tags, and event consequences, but they do not force one simulation model to solve incompatible scales.

### 4. History as a foundation, fantasy as a declared divergence

The starting world uses historical geography, peoples, polities, technologies, and constraints wherever practical. Magic, creatures, artifacts, and invented people are explicit divergence layers. The game should never present a fantasy invention as an unmarked historical fact.

### 5. Systems produce stories

Authored content defines strong situations, factions, missions, events, and constraints. Systemic interactions determine many outcomes. Procedural generation chooses from authored structures; it does not emit unrestricted lore or mechanically invalid content.

### 6. Readable strategy before spectacle

The player must understand why an outcome occurred. Every important number needs a traceable breakdown, every order a preview, every battle a readable line of command, and every loss a causal report.

## Player loop

```text
observe → form intent → issue orders → commit → watch deterministic resolution →
inspect consequences → adapt
```

The same loop repeats at different scales:

- World: choose policy and war aims.
- Region: allocate governors, supply, construction, and armies.
- Army: organize formations, officers, posture, and route.
- Battle: choose deployment, orders, reserves, and reactions.
- Party: choose movement, action, target, equipment, and risk.

## Emotional targets

- The satisfaction of building a durable state.
- The tension of incomplete information.
- The pride of an army with a history.
- The cost of distant political decisions made visible in local lives.
- The discovery of a world where myth may be materially true.
- The clarity of knowing that victory came from planning rather than opaque bonuses.

## Anti-pillars

The project is not:

- A per-soldier life simulation.
- A real-time click-speed game.
- A full physics sandbox.
- A collection of unrelated minigames.
- A generic medieval fantasy world wearing Roman visual labels.
- A historical encyclopedia with no compelling decisions.
- An AI-generated content firehose.
